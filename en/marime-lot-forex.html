How to calculate lot size (position sizing) in Forex

Published by Marius · October 15, 2026

Lot size (also called position sizing) decides how much you lose if the trade reaches the stop loss. Many beginners pick a lot size “by eye”, and a single bad trade can take a large part of the account. The correct method works the other way round: first you decide how much you are willing to risk, then you calculate the lot size that respects that amount.

Why lot size matters

The stop loss tells you how many pips you can lose, but not how much money. The money depends on the lot size: the same 25-pip stop loss can mean a small loss or a very large one, depending on the position size. That is why risk management starts with the lot size, not with the direction you think price will take. The basic rule in the ten rules is to risk at most 1–2% of the account on one trade.

Why a fixed percentage instead of a random amount

A fixed percentage adapts to the size of the account on its own and helps you survive a losing streak. The arithmetic: with 1% risk per trade, ten losses in a row leave the account about 9.6% smaller. With 10% risk, the same ten losses would reduce it by about 65%. Nobody can promise you will not have a losing streak, so a small lot size is your protection.

There is no percentage that suits everyone. The rule on this site is 1–2% at most, and at the start you can begin with 0.5–1% on a demo account. Raise the percentage only after you have followed the plan for a long time. The percentage applies to the current balance, so the lot size is recalculated on every trade, not copied from the previous one.

The lot size formula

The calculation has two steps. First, you find out how much you are willing to lose in money. Second, you divide that amount by the loss of one standard lot at the chosen stop loss.

  1. Risk in money = account balance × risk percentage.
  2. Lot size = risk in money ÷ (stop loss in pips × value of one pip for 1 lot).

Pip value basics

A pip is the smallest “standard” price move: 0.0001 on most pairs and 0.01 on yen pairs. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency. The pip value shows how much you gain or lose for a one-pip move.

  • On pairs quoted in USD, such as EURUSD, one pip on 1 lot is worth 10 USD. On 0.1 lots it is worth 1 USD, and on 0.01 lots (micro lot) 0.10 USD.
  • On yen pairs, one pip on 1 lot is 1,000 JPY, which you convert to your account currency at the current rate, so the value in USD or EUR changes.
  • On other pairs, the value is expressed in the quote currency and depends on the exchange rate. Check the contract specifications with your broker, especially for gold or indices.

Chapter 3 of the course explains pips, lots and costs in more detail.

A teaching example with a made-up rate for a yen pair: suppose USDJPY is at 150. One pip on 1 lot is 1,000 JPY ÷ 150 ≈ 6.67 USD. With a risk of 50 USD and a 30-pip stop loss, the lot size is 50 ÷ (30 × 6.67) = 0.25 lots. The real rate changes, so the pip value changes too.

A worked example, step by step

Teaching example

All the numbers below are made up, only to show the calculation. The account has 5,000 USD, the chosen risk is 1%, the pair is EURUSD and the stop loss is 25 pips.

  1. Risk in money: 5,000 × 1% = 50 USD.
  2. Loss on 1 lot: 25 pips × 10 USD = 250 USD.
  3. Lot size: 50 ÷ 250 = 0.20 lots.
  4. Check: 0.20 × 25 pips × 10 USD = 50 USD, exactly the planned risk.

The same risk with a 50-pip stop loss: 50 ÷ (50 × 10) = 0.10 lots. A wider stop loss leads to a smaller lot size, and the loss in money stays the same.

Rounding and the minimum lot

Brokers usually allow steps of 0.01 lots. Always round down so you do not exceed your risk. A teaching example with other made-up numbers: a 500 USD account, 1% risk = 5 USD, a 40-pip stop loss on EURUSD. The calculation gives 5 ÷ (40 × 10) = 0.0125 lots, so you round to 0.01. The real risk is 0.01 × 40 × 10 = 4 USD, lower than 5 USD.

If the result were below the broker’s minimum lot, do not raise the risk to make it fit. Choose a tighter stop loss only if it makes sense on the chart, or skip the trade.

Use the calculator

You do not have to do the maths by hand every time. The risk and lot calculator on this site asks for your balance, the risk percentage, the pair and the stop loss in pips, then shows the lot size. Try the example above to check that you get the same result, then use it with your own numbers. The risk/reward ratio is there too.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing the lot size before the stop loss. The correct order: the chart structure (see the plans on the patterns page) gives the stop loss, and the stop loss gives the lot size.
  • Confusing risk with margin. Margin is the amount the broker locks to keep the position open, not how much you can lose.
  • Forgetting that leverage does not change the formula, but makes it easy to open a lot size that is far too big.
  • Ignoring costs: the spread and slippage can make the real loss slightly larger than the calculated one.
  • Raising the risk after a few wins or “doubling” it after a loss to recover. Keep the risk constant.

In short

Decide the risk in percent, set the stop loss from the chart structure, calculate the lot size and round down. Then follow the plan. Risk management does not guarantee profits, but it limits your loss on every trade. If you are just starting, also read what swing trading is and how to place a stop loss correctly.
