What is swing trading and how to get started

Published by Marius · October 15, 2026

Swing trading is a trading style where you hold a position from a few days to a few weeks, trying to capture part of a price move (a “swing”). You don’t sit in front of the chart all day and you don’t chase moves lasting a few minutes. Below you will see what it means in practice, which timeframes are used, its pros and cons, and how to build a first plan.

What swing trading is

Price does not rise or fall in a straight line. It moves in waves: an impulse in one direction, a pullback, then another impulse. A swing trader follows these waves on slower charts and enters when a clear setup appears, for example a bounce from support or a break of an important level. The position stays open until price reaches the target or the stop loss.

To see how it differs from other styles, compare how long a position stays open:

  • Scalping: from seconds to a few minutes, many trades per day.
  • Day trading: positions are closed within the same day.
  • Swing trading: positions last from a few days to a few weeks.

Timeframes: H4 and D1

A timeframe is the duration of one candle: on H4 a candle covers four hours, on D1 a full day. Swing trading usually relies on these two intervals, sometimes complemented by W1 (weekly) for the big picture. You can read more about candles in chapter 2 of the course.

How to use them together

A common approach is top-down analysis. On D1 you set the context: whether the trend is up, down or sideways, and where the important supports and resistances are. On H4 you look for the entry moment: a reversal candle or a pattern at a level you already marked. This way you do not decide based on a single isolated candle.

Pros and cons

Advantages

  • Time: you need a few minutes a day to check the charts, so it can be combined with a job or studies.
  • Less noise: on larger charts, small fluctuations matter less than on minute charts.
  • Lower relative costs: the spread is a smaller part of the move you are targeting.
  • Calm decisions: you can analyse in the evening and prepare your orders, without the pressure of live price moves.

Disadvantages

  • The position stays open overnight and over the weekend, so there is a risk of a gap and a holding cost called swap.
  • The stop loss is wider, so for the same amount at risk the lot size has to be smaller.
  • Patience: days can pass without a good setup, and the temptation to force an entry appears easily.
  • Major news can move price suddenly; check the economic calendar before holding a position through it.
  • There are no guarantees: even a good plan has losing trades, and past results promise nothing about the future.

A simple plan to get started

A written plan removes improvisation. The outline below is a teaching example you can adapt, not a recommended strategy and not a promise of results.

  1. Pick one to three major pairs to follow so you get to know how they behave.
  2. On D1, define the direction (uptrend, downtrend or sideways) and mark supports and resistances.
  3. On H4, wait for a setup at a marked level, for example a pattern from the patterns page.
  4. Before entering, define the entry price, the stop loss (beyond the structure) and the target, with a risk/reward ratio of at least 1:2.
  5. Calculate the lot size with the lot calculator, starting from a fixed risk per trade.
  6. Record the trade in the journal: the reason for entry, the result and what you learned.

Teaching example

The numbers below are made up, for illustration only. On D1, EURUSD is in an uptrend and price pulls back toward a marked support. On H4 a reversal candle appears. The plan: Buy entry, stop loss below the support at 40 pips, target at 80 pips (1:2 ratio), risk of 1% of the account.

If price reaches the target, you make roughly twice the amount you risked. If it hits the stop loss, you lose the planned amount, that is 1%. The market can always move the other way: the scenario is hypothetical and does not show what will happen in reality.

Risk in swing trading

The first rule is to risk little on every trade, usually 1–2% of the account, as the ten rules also say. Because the stop loss is wider in swing trading, do not increase the lot size to “compensate”: the lot size is calculated from the stop loss, not the other way around. The article on lot size explains how to do it step by step, and the article on stop loss shows where to place it.

Also think about losing streaks, which happen with every strategy. The arithmetic is simple: with 1% risk per trade, five losses in a row leave the account about 5% smaller; with 10% risk, the same five losses would take almost 41% of the account. A small risk gives you time to learn and correct mistakes.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Too many open trades at the same time, on pairs that move together.
  • Moving the stop loss further away when price goes against you.
  • Changing strategy after two or three losses, without having tested anything enough.
  • Using high leverage, which makes any gap or sudden move much more dangerous.

Next steps

Start with a day of study, not with real money. Go through the beginner course, check what you understood in the quiz and open a demo account to practise the plan without risk. When you are unsure about a term, look it up in the glossary. Only after you have followed the plan for a while on demo does it make sense to think about small real amounts.
