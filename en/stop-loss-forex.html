Stop loss: how to place it correctly

Published by Marius · October 15, 2026

A stop loss is the order that automatically closes your trade if price reaches a level you set, limiting the loss. Almost every beginner knows they should use one, but few know where to put it. A stop loss that is too tight knocks you out of good trades, and one placed at random does not really protect you. Below you will find a simple method based on chart structure.

What a stop loss actually does

A stop loss is not a “how much it hurts” threshold. It is the point where your trade idea is proven wrong. If you bought because price bounced from a support, your idea no longer holds once that support breaks. That is where the stop loss belongs, not at a distance chosen by what your wallet feels like. Chapter 5 of the course explains order types.

Structure-based stop loss

Structure means the levels the chart has already respected: previous lows and highs, supports and resistances. The general rule:

  • On a Buy trade, the stop loss goes below the last important low or below the support you relied on.
  • On a Sell trade, the stop loss goes above the last important high or above the resistance.
  • Add a small safety margin beyond the level, so the spread or a small overshoot does not knock you out.
  • Only after you have the stop loss do you calculate the lot size, with the formula from the article on lot size.

The order matters: the structure decides the stop loss, and the stop loss decides the lot size. If the distance to the logical level is too large for your risk, the lot size shrinks or the trade is skipped. The stop loss is not tightened so that you can use a larger lot.

Why not a fixed number of pips

A fixed number of pips for every trade does not work, because a 20-pip stop loss ignores context: a more restless pair or a larger timeframe needs more distance than a quiet pair or a small timeframe. The distance comes from the chart, not from a “favourite” number.

The ATR idea: allow for market noise

ATR (Average True Range) is an indicator that shows how far price moves on average per candle, usually calculated over 14 periods. It does not tell you the direction, only how restless the market is. When ATR is high, price swings a lot and a tight stop loss is easily knocked out by noise. When it is low, you can use a closer stop loss.

A practical use: you keep the stop loss on the structure and add a safety margin equal to a fraction of the ATR (for example a quarter or a half), instead of picking a random number of pips. There is no “magic” multiplier; try it on the simulator and see what suits your style.

Teaching example

The numbers are made up, for illustration only. You want a Buy on EURUSD, on H4. The last important low is 35 pips below the entry price. ATR(14) on H4 is 32 pips. You add a margin of a quarter of the ATR, that is 8 pips, so the stop loss sits 35 + 8 = 43 pips from the entry.

With a 43-pip stop loss, the minimum target for a 1:2 ratio is 86 pips. If there is no logical room on the chart up to that point, the trade is skipped.

The link to a minimum 1:2 risk/reward ratio

The stop loss defines your unit of risk (1R). The target should be at least 2R, that is a risk/reward ratio of at least 1:2. The arithmetic shows why: at 1:2, one winning trade covers two losing ones, and the break-even point (before costs) is a win rate of about 33%. You can check the numbers with the R:R calculator.

On the patterns page, every shape has a trade plan with entry, stop loss and take profit, and the target only counts if it offers at least 2R. You can apply the same filter to any idea: if the logical stop loss and the logical target do not give 1:2, you do not enter.

When to move the stop loss

You can move the stop loss to the entry price (break-even) or follow it with a trailing stop, but only after price has moved in your favour and formed a new structural level. If you move it too early, a normal pullback takes you out at zero from a trade that could have reached the target. Decide the rule beforehand and write it in your plan.

Common stop loss mistakes

  • Not using a stop loss at all and “waiting for it to come back”. A single trade held that way can wipe out many gains.
  • Moving it further away when price gets close. You only move it in the direction of reducing risk, for example with a trailing stop once the trade is already in profit.
  • A stop loss that is too tight, inside the normal noise of the market, which knocks you out even when the idea was right.
  • A stop loss placed exactly on an obvious level, with no margin: the spread or a small overshoot can reach it.
  • Choosing the distance by amount (“I can afford 50 USD, so 10 pips”) instead of taking it from the chart and adjusting the lot size.
  • Believing the stop loss guarantees the exact loss. On news or over the weekend a gap or slippage can occur, and the order is executed at a worse price.

Checklist before entering

  1. Where is my idea wrong? That is where the stop loss is.
  2. Did I add a reasonable margin for spread and noise (for example, a fraction of ATR)?
  3. Does the target offer at least 2R up to the next logical level?
  4. Did I calculate the lot size from the stop loss and the 1–2% risk?
  5. Did I enter the stop loss in the platform before doing anything else?

A stop loss does not guarantee profits; it limits your loss on every trade. If you want to understand the context better, also read what swing trading is, go through the basic rules and practise the plan on a demo account.
