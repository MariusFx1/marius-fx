Forex, the broker and the platform

Forex (from Foreign Exchange) is the market where currencies are exchanged for one another. It's the largest financial market in the world and runs around the clock, Monday to Friday.

Currency pairs

In Forex you always trade one currency against another, which is why prices come in pairs: EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDJPY.

EURbase currency
/
USDquote currency
=
1.10001 euro costs 1.10 dollars

The price tells you how many units of the quote currency one unit of the base currency costs.

  • Buy (long): you expect the price to rise.
  • Sell (short): you expect the price to fall.

At the beginning, stick to a few heavily traded pairs, such as EURUSD, GBPUSD or USDJPY. They have low costs and it's easy to find information about them. Gold and exotic pairs move more abruptly and cost more.

Bid, ask and spread

  • Bid: the price at which you can sell.
  • Ask: the price at which you can buy, always slightly higher.

The difference between them is the spread, the main cost of a trade. That's why every position starts slightly in the red.

Example

EURUSD: bid 1.1000, ask 1.1001 → spread = 0.0001 = 1 pip

The broker and the platform

You don't trade directly on the interbank market, but through a broker. You open an account, deposit money and get access to a trading platform. The most widely used are MetaTrader 4 and MetaTrader 5 (MT4/MT5), on desktop and on mobile.

At brokers for retail clients, you usually don't buy actual currency but a CFD: a contract that tracks the price. You win or lose the price difference, with leverage (chapter 4).

Brokers also offer a demo account, with virtual money and real prices. That's where you learn the platform without any risk. Start there.

When the market moves

The market opens on Sunday evening (17:00 New York time) and closes on Friday at 17:00 New York time. Roughly, in each centre's local time:

  • Asia (Sydney, Tokyo)during the European night and early morning. Usually calmer moves.
  • London08:00–17:00 London time, the highest liquidity of the day.
  • New York08:00–17:00 New York time.
  • London–New York overlap13:00–17:00 London time, usually the most active period.

The US and Europe change their clocks on different dates, so for a few weeks in March and from late October the gap between London and New York is one hour shorter than usual. The Sessions page takes this into account and shows everything in your time.

Remember

  • Buy means you expect the price to rise, sell that you expect it to fall.
  • You buy at the ask and sell at the bid. The spread is a cost on every trade.
  • You trade through a broker, on a platform such as MT4 or MT5. Start on a demo account.
  • The most active period is usually the London–New York overlap, 13:00–17:00 London time.

The chart: candlesticks and timeframes

Before any strategy, you need to know how to read a chart. The most widely used is the candlestick chart.

Each candlestick shows what the price did over a period: where it started (open), where it ended up (close) and how high and how low it went (high and low).

  • Green candle: the close is above the open; the price rose over that period.
  • Red candle: the close is below the open; the price fell.
  • The wicks show how far the price went, even if it didn't stay there.

Over time, candlesticks form structures that are easy to recognise. On the Patterns page you'll find the best-known ones.

Timeframes

The same pair can be viewed on different timeframes: M15 (one candle = 15 minutes), H1 (one hour), H4 (4 hours), D1 (one day), W1 (one week). Small timeframes have a lot of "noise" and false signals. On the big ones, the direction is much clearer.

I'm a swing trader: I hold positions from a few days to a few weeks and work mainly on D1 and H4. I start from the higher timeframe to see the direction, then drop down to look for the entry. For a beginner, this pace is more forgiving: you have time to think.

Remember

  • A candlestick shows the open, close, high and low for a period.
  • Green = close above the open. Red = close below the open.
  • The higher timeframes (D1, H4) have less noise. Work from the top down.

Chapter 3 of 8

Pips, lots and costs

To know how much you're risking, you need to know what a price move is worth. Everything that follows builds on this chapter.

The pip

  • On most pairs, 1 pip = 0.0001 (the fourth decimal).
  • On yen (JPY) pairs, 1 pip = 0.01 (the second decimal).

Platforms show one extra decimal, called a point: 10 points = 1 pip. In MetaTrader, distances often appear in points.

EURUSD1,10357
USDJPY150,257

5 the pip digit7 the point (1/10 pip)

The lot

Position size is chosen in lots:

TypeLotsUnits1 pip on EURUSD
Standard1,00100.00010 USD
Mini0,1010.0001 USD
Micro0,011.0000,10 USD

With 0.01 lots (usually the minimum size) you can start on a small live account, with controlled risk.

Pip value

Pip value = pip size × number of units

Example · EURUSD

1 lot: 0.0001 × 100,000 = 10 USD per pip

You buy 0.10 lots (1 USD per pip) at 1.1000 and close at 1.1035, which is 35 pips:

35 × 1 USD = 35 USD

If the price had fallen 35 pips, you would have lost the same 35 USD. For a euro account, you divide by the exchange rate. At EURUSD = 1.10:

10 USD ÷ 1.10 ≈ €9.09 per pip for 1 lot

On other instruments the value differs: on USDJPY, one pip on 1 lot means 1,000 JPY, which at a rate of 150 is about 1,000 ÷ 150 ≈ 6.67 USD. On gold, one lot is usually 100 ounces, so a 1-dollar move means 100 USD on 1 lot. Check the symbol specifications with your broker. The lot size calculator does the conversions for you.

The costs of a trade

  • The spread, on every entry. On EURUSD, with a 1-pip spread and 0.10 lots: 1 USD. It widens around news, at night and when the market reopens.
  • Commission: some accounts have a tighter spread but a fixed commission per lot. Compare the total cost.
  • Swap: if you hold the position overnight, you pay a cost (sometimes you receive a small gain), based on the interest rates of the two currencies. At most brokers it is charged triple on Wednesdays, to cover the weekend. It matters for swing trading.

Remember

  • 1 pip = 0.0001 on most pairs and 0.01 on JPY pairs. 10 points = 1 pip.
  • 1 lot = 100,000 units. On EURUSD, 1 lot = 10 USD per pip, 0.01 lots = 0.10 USD per pip.
  • The real costs are the spread, commission and swap. Take them into account.

Chapter 4 of 8

Leverage and margin

Leverage draws many people to Forex. It's also the reason many of them empty their accounts.

Leverage lets you open a position much larger than the money in your account. With 1:30 leverage, for every €1 in your account you can control a €30 position. The broker locks up part of your account as collateral, and this is called margin.

Required margin = position value ÷ leverage

Important: profit and loss are calculated on the full value of the position, not on the margin. Leverage magnifies losses just as much.

Example · €1,000 account, 1:30 leverage

You buy 0.10 lots of EURUSD. Position value: €10,000.

Margin = 10,000 ÷ 30 ≈ €333.33 · 100 pips against you ≈ 100 × €0.909 ≈ €90.91 (9% of the account)

For 1 lot you would need €3,333.33 of margin, so you can't open it. The limit protects you. At a broker outside the EU, with 1:500 leverage, the margin for 1 lot would be only €200:

1 lot: 100 pips against you ≈ 100 × €9.09 ≈ €909, which is 91% of the account

Margin call and stop out

Margin level = equity ÷ margin used × 100%

  • Margin call: the warning that your free margin has run out. The level is set by the broker.
  • Stop out: the broker closes your positions automatically. In the EU, for retail clients, ESMA rules require positions to be closed when equity falls below 50% of the initial margin required. Outside the EU, the broker chooses the level. If you get here, you've risked far too much.

Protections in the EU

At EU-regulated brokers, retail clients have maximum leverage capped by ESMA (these limits apply to EU clients):

InstrumentMaximum leverage
Pairs made up only of USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, CAD, CHF (e.g. EURUSD, USDJPY)1:30
Other pairs (e.g. AUDUSD), gold, major indices1:20
Other commodities (e.g. oil), other indices1:10
Shares1:5
Cryptocurrencies1:2

Also in the EU, retail clients have negative balance protection: you can't lose more than the money in your account. Brokers outside the EU aren't required to apply these rules, so check which of the broker's entities opens your account.

Remember

  • Leverage magnifies gains and losses equally.
  • Margin is collateral locked up by the broker, not your maximum loss.
  • Stop out closes positions automatically. In the EU, at 50% of the required margin.
  • You size your lot by risk, not by how much leverage allows.

Chapter 5 of 8

Orders, stop loss and take profit

If you only remember one thing from this whole lesson, I'd like it to be this: no trade without a stop loss.

Order types

  • Market: you enter immediately, at the current price.
  • Limit: you enter at a better price. Buy limit below the current price, sell limit above it.
  • Stop: you enter when the price confirms the direction. Buy stop above the current price, sell stop below it.

A stop loss that gets hit fills at the first available price. Around news or after the weekend, the price can jump past your level (a gap) and you exit at a worse price than planned (slippage). The SL limits your loss, but it doesn't guarantee it to the pip.

Stop loss: always

The stop loss (SL) closes your position automatically at a price you set in advance. Without an SL, a single unexpected news release, or one evening where you tell yourself "leave it, it'll come back", can wipe out months of work.

The SL goes where your idea is proven wrong, not at a number of pips picked at random:

  • On a buy: below the last relevant low or below a support zone.
  • On a sell: above the last relevant high or above a resistance.
  • Leave a few pips of buffer, plus the spread.

Only then do you calculate the lot size (chapter 6). The chart dictates the distance, and the lot adapts to it.

Golden rule: never move the SL further away when the price gets close to it. You may only move it to reduce your risk, for example to your entry price, and only if your plan says so.

Take profit and the risk/reward ratio

The take profit (TP) closes your position automatically at your target. R:R compares how much you risk with how much you can make. The risk on one trade is called 1R.

Example

Buy EURUSD at 1.1000, SL at 1.0975, TP at 1.1050.

Risk 25 pips · target 50 pips → R:R = 1:2

With an R:R of 1:2 you need to win at least one trade in three to break even, before costs: 1 ÷ (1 + 2) = 33.3%. At 1:1 you need more than 50%.

10 trades, 4 won and 6 lost: 4 × 2R − 6 × 1R = +2R

This is a calculation, not a promise: the ratio matters more than how often you're right.

The TP goes in a logical place, before a zone where the price may turn around, not just to get a big ratio.

Remember

  • Market = now, limit = at a better price, stop = on confirmation.
  • No trade without a stop loss, placed where the idea is proven wrong.
  • Never move the SL further away.
  • Look for an R:R of at least 1:2, with logical targets.

Chapter 6 of 8

Risk management

Your strategy gives you entries. Risk management keeps you in the game long enough to learn.

The 1% rule

You risk a maximum of 1% of your account per trade. On €1,000, that means a loss of no more than €10 if your stop loss is hit. It looks small, and that's exactly the point: you can be wrong many times without being knocked out of the game. When you're starting out, even 0.5% is a very good choice.

Calculating the lot size

Lot = amount at risk ÷ (stop loss in pips × pip value for 1 lot)

Example · €1,000 account, 1% risk, 25-pip SL, EURUSD

  1. Amount at risk€1,000 × 1% = €10
  2. Stop loss25 pips, below the last low
  3. Pip value for 1 lot, in euros10 USD ÷ 1.10 ≈ €9.09
  4. Lot10 ÷ (25 × 9.09) ≈ 0.044
  5. Round down0.04 lots

Check: 0.04 × 25 × 9.09 ≈ €9.09, below €10. Always round down, otherwise you risk more than 1%.

If the chart calls for a 50-pip SL, the lot becomes 0.02. The risk in euros stays the same; only the position size changes. In the calculator, with EURUSD, currency EUR, balance 1000, risk 1, stop loss 25 and price 1.10, you also get 0.04 lots.

Losing streaks are normal

Losing streaks will come even with a good method. Strictly mathematically, at a 50% win rate, the chance of at least 6 losses in a row over 100 trades is about 55%.

Big losses are hard to recover

LossGain needed to get back
−10%+11,1%
−20%+25%
−30%+42,9%
−50%+100%
−75%+300%
−90%+900%

From €1,000 you lose 50% and are left with €500. To get back to €1,000 you need +€500, which is +100% of what you have left.

Also set yourself a daily and weekly loss limit, for example 2% and 5%. When you hit it, close the platform.

Remember

  • A maximum of 1% risk per trade. At the beginning, 0.5% is even better.
  • Lot = amount at risk ÷ (SL in pips × pip value per 1 lot). Round down.
  • Losing streaks are normal. Small risk makes them bearable.

Chapter 7 of 8

Psychology, discipline and mistakes

You can learn a strategy in a few weeks. Discipline takes years. Many accounts are lost not for lack of knowledge, but because of emotions.

The trading plan

A written document, not one in your head. When emotions run high, the plan does the thinking for you. It has to answer:

  • What you trade (2–3 pairs) and at what times.
  • What exactly you need to see on the chart to enter.
  • Where you place your SL and TP.
  • How much you risk per trade, per day and per week.
  • What you do after a loss or a losing streak.

The journal

Log every trade: the pair, the reason for entering, SL, TP, the result in R, how you felt and whether you followed your plan. After 20–30 trades you'll see what works and where you go wrong. There's a free journal on this site, saved only in your browser.

The emotional traps

  • Revenge trading: after a loss you want to "win it back" right away, usually with a bigger lot. Antidote: after 2 losses in a row, close the platform until the next day.
  • FOMO (fear of missing out): you jump late into a big move, without a logical SL. Antidote: a missed entry isn't a loss. Other opportunities will come.
  • Overtrading: too many trades, out of boredom. Antidote: only enter the setups in your plan.
  • Fear and greed: you close winners too early and let losers run. Antidote: SL and TP set from the start and left alone.

Typical mistakes

Besides the ones about stop loss and risk, here are the mistakes I made myself at the beginning:

  1. Adding to a losing position

    Never "average down" a loss.

  2. Changing strategy after every loss

    Test it on at least 30–50 demo trades before you judge it.

  3. Trading the news without knowing it

    Check the economic calendar every day (interest rates, inflation, US jobs).

  4. Copying signals without understanding them

    If you don't know why you entered, you won't know when to get out either.

Remember

  • A written plan before the first trade, a journal entry after each one.
  • After losses: a break, not revenge.
  • The big mistakes usually come from too much risk and no rules.

Chapter 8 of 8

Your first concrete steps

You've reached the end. Here's exactly what I would do if I were starting from zero today.

  1. Master the calculations

    Reread chapters 3–6 until you can work out the lot size yourself. Use the calculator to check.

  2. Demo account, at least 6–8 weeks

    And a minimum of 30–50 trades, with the same 1% risk, the same plan and a complete journal, as if it were real money. Before or alongside that, practise on historical data in the backtesting simulator: you see the chart bar by bar, without knowing what comes next.

  3. Write your plan

    Start from the basic rules. Choose 2–3 pairs, the hours you'll trade (see Sessions) and 1–2 setups you understand, for example from the patterns.

  4. A journal from your first trade

    Log everything in the journal and read it every weekend.

  5. Go live only if you've been disciplined

    Not "if you made a profit on demo", but if you followed your plan. Start with a small account, with money you can afford to lose, and with 0.01 lots.

  6. Choose your broker carefully

    Check the regulation, the costs and how easily you can withdraw your money. On the Broker page you'll find the broker I use (affiliate link).

Checklist before every trade

If a single point is missing, you don't enter.

Tick all 8 points before you enter.

Trading isn't a race. What matters isn't how fast you get there, but getting there with your account intact and a process you can repeat. If you have questions, join the free Telegram community or write to me.
